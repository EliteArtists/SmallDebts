Section 21 — 7 Reasons It Might Be Invalid

If your landlord served you a Section 21 notice before 1 May 2026, it may already be invalid — and you may have no legal obligation to leave. Here are the seven grounds most commonly used to challenge one, and what to do if any of them apply to you.

Important: Section 21 "no-fault" eviction notices were abolished in the private rented sector on 1 May 2026 under the Renters' Rights Act 2025. No new Section 21 notices can be served from that date. However, notices served before 1 May 2026 can still proceed through the courts — and many are currently doing so. If you received a Section 21 notice before that date and proceedings are ongoing, the grounds below may still apply to your situation.

Why Section 21 Was So Easy to Get Wrong

Section 21 appeared straightforward on the surface — a landlord gives two months' notice, no reason required, tenant must leave. In practice, a valid Section 21 required a landlord to have ticked every item on a compliance checklist before the notice was even handed over. A single failure on that list was enough to invalidate the entire notice.

Courts do not overlook these failures. If the conditions were not met, the notice is invalid and any possession order sought on the basis of it will be refused. The seven grounds below are the ones that came up most often — in Facebook groups, in our own caseload, and in the courts.

The 7 Grounds

1. The Deposit Was Not Protected

A landlord cannot serve a valid Section 21 notice unless the deposit has been registered with one of the three government-approved tenancy deposit protection schemes — the DPS, MyDeposits, or the TDS — within 30 days of receiving it. If the deposit was never protected, or was protected late and never remedied, the notice is invalid.

The only way a landlord in this position could revive the ability to serve a Section 21 was to return the deposit in full to the tenant first. If that did not happen before the notice was served, the notice does not stand.

2. The Prescribed Information Was Not Provided

Protecting the deposit is not enough on its own. The landlord must also serve the tenant with written "prescribed information" — a document confirming which scheme holds the deposit, how to recover it, and how disputes are resolved. This must be provided within 30 days of the deposit being received.

Many landlords protected the deposit correctly but never provided the prescribed information, or provided an incomplete version of it. That is treated in law as equivalent to failing to protect the deposit at all. The Section 21 notice served in those circumstances is invalid.

3. A New Landlord Did Not Re-Register the Deposit

When a property is sold and a new landlord takes over mid-tenancy, the deposit protection obligation transfers with it. The new landlord inherits the tenancy — including all compliance requirements. If the previous landlord's deposit protection lapsed, or the new landlord failed to re-register and re-serve prescribed information in their own name, any Section 21 they subsequently served was invalid.

This catches a significant number of landlords who bought tenanted properties and assumed the previous owner's compliance transferred automatically. It does not.

4. No Valid Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) Was Provided

Landlords are required to provide tenants with a valid Energy Performance Certificate before or at the start of the tenancy. The EPC must cover the whole property being let and must have been current at the time it was provided. If no EPC was given, or if the one provided had expired, a Section 21 notice served afterwards is invalid.

The landlord must have a record of having provided the EPC — a signed receipt or a sent email with the document attached. An EPC that exists on file but was never handed over does not satisfy the requirement.

5. No Valid Gas Safety Certificate Was Provided

Where a property has gas appliances, the landlord must provide the tenant with a current gas safety certificate carried out by a Gas Safe registered engineer. This must be given before the tenancy begins, or within 28 days of the annual check being completed. A Section 21 notice is invalid if the tenant was never given a valid gas safety certificate.

A certificate that was completed but not handed to the tenant is not sufficient. The obligation is to provide it — not merely to have it. Recent case law, including Cassell v Sidhu (2025), has confirmed that even technical defects in a gas safety certificate — such as a missing landlord address — can place the validity of a subsequent Section 21 in question.

6. The Wrong Form Was Used or the Notice Period Was Incorrect

For all tenancies created on or after 1 October 2015, a Section 21 notice must be served on the prescribed government form — Form 6A. Using an older version of the form, a self-drafted letter, or any unofficial document in its place renders the notice invalid.

The notice period must also be correct. For most assured shorthold tenancies, the minimum notice period is two months. Notices that give less than two months, or that set an expiry date that falls within the first four months of the original tenancy, are invalid. The expiry date must also align correctly with the tenancy period — an error here, even a small one, is a valid ground of challenge.

7. The Notice Was Served in Retaliation for a Complaint

Under the Deregulation Act 2015, a Section 21 notice is invalid if it was served after a tenant made a written complaint about the condition of the property and the landlord either failed to respond adequately within 14 days, or responded to the complaint by serving the notice.

If the tenant then escalated the complaint to the local authority and an improvement notice or emergency remedial notice was subsequently issued, the landlord is barred from serving a Section 21 for six months from the date of that notice. Any notice served within that window is automatically invalid.

The key requirement is that the tenant's original complaint was made in writing. A verbal complaint does not engage these protections — but a text message, WhatsApp message, or email does.

What To Do If Any of These Apply

If you believe your Section 21 notice is invalid on any of the grounds above, do not leave the property simply because the notice date has passed. An invalid notice gives the landlord no right to possession. They would need to start again — and with Section 21 now abolished, that is no longer an option.

Your immediate steps should be:

  1. Check all three deposit protection schemes to confirm whether your deposit was registered — depositprotection.com, mydeposits.co.uk, tenancydepositscheme.com
  2. Review what documents you were given at the start of the tenancy — EPC, gas safety certificate, prescribed deposit information, How to Rent guide
  3. Check the notice itself — is it on Form 6A? Does the notice period add up correctly? Are all tenant names listed accurately?
  4. If you made any written complaints about repairs before the notice was served, preserve those messages as evidence
  5. Send a formal written response to your landlord setting out the specific grounds on which you consider the notice invalid

When to Act Quickly

Act immediately if any of the following apply:

  • Your landlord has already applied to the court for a possession order
  • You have received a court date or hearing notice
  • Your landlord is threatening to change the locks or remove your belongings
  • You complained about repairs in writing shortly before the notice arrived
  • You were never given an EPC, gas certificate, or prescribed deposit information
